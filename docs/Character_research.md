# Character challenge research

Research date: 2026-10-31

## Purpose and scope

This document collects research and product implications for Daily Forge's
**Character** challenges. The requested emphasis is Stoic philosophy, especially
practical exercises that help a person become more prudent, temperate,
courageous, just, patient, honest, generous, and humble through ordinary daily
choices.

The intended program is not a course in ancient philosophy and is not a system
for scoring whether the user is a good person. It should help the user practice
better judgment and action in situations that are substantially under their own
control, reflect privately, and build a more reliable disposition over time.

This is product research, not mental-health treatment, spiritual direction, or
proof that one philosophical school has resolved every ethical question. The
app must not diagnose the user, suppress legitimate emotion, prescribe unsafe
hardship, or make important medical, legal, relational, or professional
decisions on the user's behalf.

## Executive recommendation: the Character “gold find”

The closest Character equivalent to Peter Attia's Centenarian Decathlon and
Harvard Project Zero's Studio Thinking framework is:

> **Epictetus's three disciplines—desire, action, and assent—organized around
> the four Stoic virtues of practical wisdom, justice, courage, and temperance,
> and delivered as small repeated exercises with morning preparation and evening
> review.**

This recommendation deliberately combines three related layers rather than
forcing one ancient source to do every job:

1. **Epictetus supplies the educational program.** His three fields of training
   identify what a developing person actually practices: placing desire well,
   choosing appropriate action, and examining impressions before assent.
2. **The four Stoic virtues supply the capability map.** Practical wisdom,
   justice, courage, and temperance prevent a narrow program concerned only with
   calm or self-control. In Stoicism, character is rational, social, and active.
3. **Stoic spiritual exercises supply the daily form.** Epictetus, Seneca, and
   Marcus Aurelius show philosophy being rehearsed, applied, and reviewed.
   Modern Stoicism's Stoic Week demonstrates that these ideas can be presented
   as a bounded routine of morning preparation, a daily theme, and evening
   reflection.

The principal person is therefore **Epictetus**, not because Daily Forge should
copy every ancient belief, but because his teaching is unusually close to an
actionable curriculum for character development. **Stoic Week** is the best
modern program precedent found for translating the curriculum into daily
practice. **Pierre Hadot's philosophy-as-a-way-of-life interpretation** is a
useful scholarly bridge: the point is not to collect sayings but to use
repeated exercises to change how one sees, chooses, and acts.

## Why Epictetus is the strongest anchor

Epictetus distinguishes book learning from “education for living.” The Stanford
Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes his central concerns as integrity,
self-management, personal freedom, volition, and the proper use of impressions.
His school did teach texts and logic, but he repeatedly treated intellectual
knowledge as instrumental to acquiring habits that enable better conduct.

That makes his framework unusually suitable for Daily Forge. A challenge can
be more than a quotation or journal prompt: it can exercise a named capacity,
produce a small observable action, and invite a short private review.

In _Discourses_ 3.2, Epictetus identifies three fields in which a person who
wants to become good must train:

| Discipline          | Ancient concern                                                                             | Daily Forge translation                                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Desire and aversion | Wanting and avoiding the right things; not making one's moral purpose depend on externals   | Notice when peace or self-respect has been made conditional on another person's response or an uncertain outcome; redirect attention to a chosen judgment or action |
| Action and impulse  | Acting or refraining appropriately, with care, in one's relationships and roles             | Identify the role and value at stake, then perform one fair, helpful, honest, or responsible action that is actually the user's to take                             |
| Assent              | Testing impressions instead of accepting a first appearance or value judgment automatically | Pause, separate observation from interpretation, consider alternatives, and choose whether a judgment deserves assent                                               |

These are not independent personality traits. A person may need to inspect an
impression, regulate a desire, and then act justly in one episode. The framework
should therefore classify challenges by a **primary discipline** while allowing
secondary disciplines and virtues.

### The discipline of desire is not “stop caring”

The practical question is not whether health, family, livelihood, reputation,
or social outcomes matter. Stoics classified many such things as “preferred
indifferents”: worth pursuing, but not the final measure of moral worth.
Epictetus's learner still undertakes projects and meets obligations. The
training is to avoid making the quality of one's choice depend on commanding an
outcome that no person fully controls.

Daily Forge should use the more faithful phrase **“up to me”** or
**“attributable to my choice”** more often than the popularized phrase “under my
control.” Most real situations contain influence, uncertainty, shared agency,
and chance. A useful app exercise maps:

- what is directly the user's choice now;
- what the user can influence but not guarantee; and
- what is not theirs to command.

The exercise must end with appropriate action, not passive resignation.

### The discipline of action makes Stoicism social

Epictetus explicitly says the learner should not become “unfeeling like a
statue.” Action is shaped by natural and acquired relationships—family member,
friend, neighbor, colleague, citizen—and by what is fitting in the actual
situation. His examples are culturally and historically situated, so Daily
Forge must not reproduce ancient role hierarchy as unquestionable authority.
The durable insight is that character is demonstrated in responsible relations,
not only in private composure.

The program should therefore repeatedly ask:

1. What role or commitment is active here?
2. Who could be affected?
3. What would practical wisdom and justice require?
4. What part is mine to do, with consent and appropriate boundaries?
5. Can I do it without demanding a particular response?

This protects the Character area from becoming self-optimization detached from
other people. It also gives Character a boundary with Community: Character
trains the quality of judgment and action; Community focuses on helpful action,
participation, and volunteering. One episode may support both, but it should
have one primary area.

### The discipline of assent creates concrete micro-practices

An impression is how a situation initially appears, including the value judgment
that may arrive with it: “This delay is unbearable,” “That criticism proves I
failed,” or “I must answer immediately.” Assent is accepting the impression as
true or action-guiding.

A Daily Forge assent challenge can be extremely small:

1. notice one emotionally charged or urgent impression;
2. pause without denying the emotion;
3. write the observable facts in one sentence;
4. write the first interpretation separately;
5. name one plausible alternative interpretation;
6. choose a proportionate action or intentionally defer action.

Completion means the process was attempted. The app does not decide which
interpretation is morally correct, and a pause must never be required in an
emergency where immediate protective action is appropriate.

## The four virtues as a balanced capability map

Stoicism is a form of virtue ethics. Its usual four cardinal virtues are
practical wisdom, justice, courage, and temperance. The Internet Encyclopedia of
Philosophy notes more specific capacities within them: judgment, discretion,
and resourcefulness under practical wisdom; kindness and sociability under
justice; perseverance and confidence under courage; and propriety and
self-control under temperance.

| Virtue           | Working product definition                                                                                                | Failure mode if isolated                                          |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Practical wisdom | Perceive relevant facts, weigh values and consequences, recognize uncertainty, and choose a fitting means                 | Cleverness used without justice; endless analysis with no action  |
| Justice          | Treat people fairly, honor legitimate commitments, respect consent and dignity, contribute appropriately, and repair harm | Self-sacrifice without boundaries; rule-following without context |
| Courage          | Take a fitting action despite fear, discomfort, uncertainty, or social friction                                           | Recklessness, aggression, needless risk, or performative hardship |
| Temperance       | Govern appetite, attention, speech, and impulse so that a larger purpose can guide action                                 | Suppression, rigidity, purity tests, shame, or deprivation        |

The Stoics treated the virtues as interdependent. Daily Forge may label one
primary virtue for selection and history, but it must not imply that courage
without judgment and justice is character excellence. A “courage” challenge
must still pass wisdom, consent, and safety checks.

### Practical wisdom is the coordinating capacity

Practical wisdom is not abstract intelligence. It is context-sensitive judgment
about what matters and what to do. It should appear in every challenge through
questions such as:

- What facts do I know, and what am I inferring?
- What value or commitment is actually at stake?
- What do I not know?
- Who is affected?
- What is a proportionate next action?
- What would make me reconsider?

### Justice prevents an inward-only program

Justice includes fairness, honesty, kindness, sociability, and appropriate
concern. Stoic cosmopolitanism and the tradition of _oikeiosis_ describe moral
concern expanding beyond the isolated self. The app can encourage listening,
crediting others, keeping commitments, making repairs, and widening attention
to someone routinely overlooked.

The person affected must never become a prop in the user's self-improvement.
Challenges must respect consent, privacy, power differences, and a person's
right to decline help or conversation.

### Courage should be appropriate, not maximal

Courage is acting well in the presence of fear or difficulty. It is not seeking
danger, winning a confrontation, tolerating abuse, or refusing needed help. The
program should progress from low-stakes acts such as asking a clarifying
question or admitting a minor error. It should exclude dangerous exposure,
sleep deprivation, unsafe temperature exposure, food restriction, medical
nonadherence, confrontation with an abusive person, and activities that could
threaten employment or safety without deliberate user planning.

### Temperance is flexible self-government

Temperance challenges should create a space in which choice becomes possible:
delay a nonessential impulse, stop after a chosen amount, put a device out of
reach for a bounded interval, listen before replying, or choose a planned
alternative. They should not moralize food, body size, sexuality, spending,
substance use, or medical symptoms.

Where addiction, an eating disorder, compulsive behavior, or withdrawal may be
involved, a generic daily challenge is not an appropriate intervention. The app
should offer a neutral alternative and, where relevant, encourage qualified
support without diagnosis.

## Practice, not quotation collection

The practical Stoics repeatedly distinguish knowing from doing. Epictetus
describes self-examination and correction of views; Musonius Rufus gives
practice priority over theory; Seneca reviews his conduct; Marcus Aurelius's
_Meditations_ are private reminders to apply philosophy rather than a public
treatise.

Daily Forge should use four recurring structures:

| Practice structure | Purpose                                                          | Example                                                                   |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Prepare            | Anticipate a likely situation and choose a principle or response | “Name one predictable irritation and the response you want available.”    |
| Notice             | Catch an impression, impulse, or role while it is active         | “Before one nonurgent reply, identify fact, interpretation, and impulse.” |
| Act                | Perform one bounded, values-consistent behavior                  | “Correct one small error without adding an excuse.”                       |
| Review             | Examine what happened with candor and self-respect               | “What went well, where did judgment slip, and what will you try next?”    |

These structures can occur in one day or across several days. They are not a
mandatory sequence and should not make ordinary life feel constantly audited.

## Stoic Week as a delivery precedent

Modern Stoicism's Stoic Week asks participants to “live like a Stoic” for one
week. Its handbooks use a daily theme plus morning and evening meditations. The
morning practice rehearses the day and distinguishes chosen conduct from
uncontrolled outcomes; the evening practice reviews intentions and conduct.

This is useful product precedent for three reasons:

1. it translates a philosophical system into bounded daily exercises;
2. it combines study, preparation, behavior, and reflection; and
3. it treats a short program as the beginning of practice, not mastery.

Daily Forge should adapt the rhythm without copying a seven-day course or
requiring religious or metaphysical agreement:

- optional morning intention of one or two minutes;
- one small daily practice, usually under 15 minutes of deliberate effort;
- an in-the-moment cue when the relevant situation occurs; and
- optional evening review of two or three questions.

The “daily challenge” can be completed by the deliberate attempt even when the
external opportunity never arrives. For example, preparing a fair response and
noticing that the expected conflict did not occur is still useful information.

### What the modern evidence can and cannot establish

Modern Stoicism reports repeated pre/post improvements among Stoic Week
completers in self-reported life satisfaction, positive emotion, flourishing,
and negative emotion. Its 2021 report also used the WHO-5 and found higher
end-of-week scores among respondents who completed both measurements. This is
promising evidence that people find the format usable and potentially helpful.

It is not proof that Stoic Week caused durable character change. The public
reports generally involve volunteers, self-report, attrition, and no randomized
control group for the main annual event. Participants know the program's aims,
and people who complete the follow-up may differ from people who leave. Daily
Forge should therefore cite Stoic Week as a **delivery precedent**, not as a
clinically proven character treatment.

There is a growing empirical literature, including peer-reviewed Stoic training
studies and a 2025 validation study for the Stoic Attitudes and Behaviours Scale
(SABS). SABS is useful evidence that philosophical Stoicism can be represented
more carefully than colloquial emotional suppression. It should not become a
hidden moral score in Daily Forge. A psychometric scale designed for research is
not automatically suitable for daily personalization, and high adherence to a
questionnaire is not equivalent to being a good person.

## Philosophical Stoicism is not emotional suppression

Modern English uses lowercase “stoicism” for silently enduring pain, concealing
distress, or suppressing emotion. That is not an adequate account of the ancient
philosophy. A 2022 cross-cultural study found that a lay “stoic ideology” scale
centered on endurance and non-expression was negatively associated with
well-being; the authors explicitly distinguished that construct from the
philosophical tradition. Modern Stoicism likewise describes SABS as measuring
philosophical Stoicism, not hiding distress.

Daily Forge must therefore say:

- notice and name emotion rather than deny it;
- examine the judgment and impulse accompanying it;
- choose a proportionate response;
- seek support when support is appropriate; and
- treat calm as a possible by-product, not proof of virtue.

The app should never reward the user for enduring untreated pain, staying silent
about harm, concealing a mental-health crisis, or remaining in an unsafe
situation.

## Proposed Character goals, version 0.1

These goals describe capacities the user can practice. They are not age-based
targets and not pass/fail declarations of virtue. The identifiers reserve
`CHG` for Character goals so they cannot be confused with Creativity's `CG`
identifiers.

### CHG-01 — Examine impressions before assent

Create enough space between an initial appearance and a nonurgent response to
distinguish observation, interpretation, emotion, and impulse. Consider
alternatives before accepting a value judgment as settled.

**Visible evidence:** the user can describe one event in neutral terms, identify
their first interpretation, and name the judgment or action they chose.

### CHG-02 — Place effort where agency is real

Distinguish chosen judgment and action from influence, shared agency, chance,
and other people's decisions. Pursue worthwhile external goals without making
integrity or self-respect conditional on controlling the result.

**Visible evidence:** the user can identify a next action that is theirs to take
and an outcome they will pursue without claiming to command it.

### CHG-03 — Exercise practical wisdom

Make proportionate decisions using relevant facts, values, uncertainty,
consequences, affected people, and willingness to revise.

**Visible evidence:** the user can state the decision, the main consideration,
one uncertainty, and the condition that would justify reconsideration.

### CHG-04 — Practice justice in roles and relationships

Act fairly and honestly, respect consent and dignity, keep legitimate
commitments, share credit, listen, contribute, and repair avoidable harm while
maintaining healthy boundaries.

**Visible evidence:** the user performs or prepares one specific appropriate
action. Another person's approval is not required for completion.

### CHG-05 — Act courageously at an appropriate scale

Take a prudent, values-consistent action despite fear, discomfort, uncertainty,
or social friction, without courting danger or overriding consent.

**Visible evidence:** the user attempts the named action or its preselected safer
variation and records what made it worth attempting.

### CHG-06 — Govern impulse with temperance

Create a deliberate interval between appetite or impulse and action, then choose
in light of a larger purpose rather than shame or rigid denial.

**Visible evidence:** the user notices one impulse and deliberately proceeds,
modifies, delays, or declines it.

### CHG-07 — Fulfill commitments and roles deliberately

Recognize which chosen role is active, what legitimate responsibility belongs
to it, and what “done well enough” means in the circumstances.

**Visible evidence:** the user names a role and completes one bounded duty,
renegotiates it honestly, or declines a duty that was never legitimately theirs.

### CHG-08 — Admit error, learn, and repair

Notice defensiveness, acknowledge a mistake without self-condemnation, correct
what can be corrected, and extract a specific adjustment for future action.

**Visible evidence:** an acknowledgment, repair attempt, correction, or learning
statement exists. Forgiveness and reconciliation remain outside the user's
control.

### CHG-09 — Expand appropriate concern

Notice people beyond the user's habitual circle and act with fairness,
kindness, or civic regard while preserving privacy, consent, and competence.

**Visible evidence:** the user pays fuller attention or offers an appropriate
action. An offer that is respectfully declined can count.

### CHG-10 — Build a sustainable reflective practice

Prepare for predictable situations, review conduct honestly, preserve what
worked, and select one small correction without rumination or self-punishment.

**Visible evidence:** a brief intention or review is recorded in a form the user
chooses. Missing a review carries no penalty.

## Baseline probes before personalization

Initially the app will not know which situations are difficult, which virtues
the user values most, or which challenge level is appropriate. Early Character
challenges should therefore be **probes**, just as early Body challenges gather
safe baseline information.

Probe identifiers use `PCH-XX-YY`, where `XX` identifies the corresponding
`CHG-XX` goal and `YY` identifies a particular probe. Probes should collect
experience, not grade character.

Each probe can receive:

- attempted / not attempted;
- felt easy / stretching / too much;
- opportunity occurred / did not occur;
- optional private description;
- “use again,” “make gentler,” “make more demanding,” or “do not suggest”; and
- an optional note about context or a needed variation.

| PCH-01-02 | Before one nonurgent digital reply, pause for ten seconds and name the emotion and impulse present.                                               |
| PCH-01-03 | For one mildly charged impression, name one plausible alternative explanation and then choose a response.                                         |
| PCH-01-04 | Revisit one judgment later in the day and decide whether to retain, qualify, suspend, or reject it.                                               |

## Recommended challenge families

The probes establish comfort and relevance. Later challenges should form
families that can recur with different contexts and modest progression.

### 1. Stop and test the impression

Exercises separate facts, interpretations, emotions, and impulses. Progression
comes from moving from retrospective review to an in-the-moment pause, not from
seeking more emotionally intense events.

Example challenges:

- “Catch one ‘always,’ ‘never,’ ‘must,’ or ‘disaster’ thought and rewrite it as
  a specific, testable claim.”
- “Before one nonurgent response, ask: What appeared? What did I add? What action
  fits?”
- “Hold one uncertain judgment as provisional until one more fact is known.”

### 2. Agency mapping and purposeful action

Exercises distinguish choice, influence, and outcome, then require a concrete
next action. This family must never end with “ignore what you cannot control.”

Example challenges:

- “Take one worry and convert the part attributable to you into a calendar
  action, question, or five-minute first step.”
- “Prepare carefully for one result without using the result as today's success
  criterion.”
- “When another person decides differently, identify the next fair action that
  remains yours.”

### 3. Roles, duties, and justice

Exercises connect conduct to legitimate commitments and affected people. The
user can challenge or renegotiate a role rather than treating every expectation
as binding.

Example challenges:

- “Choose one role you will inhabit today. Write what a good-enough performance
  requires and do the smallest unfinished part.”
- “Share one piece of credit specifically and accurately.”
- “Keep, renegotiate, or explicitly release one small commitment before it
  becomes a silent failure.”

### 4. Temperance as a choice interval

Exercises practice freedom from automaticity, not deprivation. Progression
increases reliability and relevance before duration.

Example challenges:

- “Delay one nonessential impulse by two minutes, then choose without shame.”
- “Use a ten-minute single-task boundary and notice each urge to switch.”
- “In one conversation, let the other person's last sentence finish before
  composing your response.”

### 5. Prudent courage

Exercises take small, worthwhile actions despite discomfort. The app should
offer a lower-intensity variation and a “not safe in this context” replacement.

Example challenges:

- “Ask the question whose answer would prevent avoidable confusion.”
- “State one respectful boundary using a short, non-accusatory sentence.”
- “Submit, request, volunteer, or begin one thing where attempting is in your
  control and acceptance is not.”

### 6. Humility, feedback, and repair

Exercises turn error into corrective action without humiliation. The person
affected controls whether to engage, forgive, or reconcile.

Example challenges:

- “Correct one error plainly. Stop after the correction unless context requires
  more.”
- “Ask for one piece of specific feedback and respond first with thanks or a
  clarifying question.”
- “Make one repair whose purpose is to address impact, not obtain reassurance.”

### 7. Expanding concern

Exercises widen attention through ordinary respect, civic learning, and
consensual help. They can lead to the Community area when volunteering or
ongoing participation becomes the primary action.

Example challenges:

- “Learn and use the preferred name of someone you routinely encounter, if the
  context makes that welcome.”
- “Offer one bounded form of help and make declining easy.”
- “Read one primary local source about an issue before forming or repeating an
  opinion.”

### 8. Morning preparation

Preparation is specific and brief. It should rehearse a response, not invite
catastrophic rumination.

Example challenge:

> Identify one ordinary difficulty likely today. Name the first impression it
> may trigger, the virtue you want available, and one sentence or action you can
> rehearse. Then return to the day.

### 9. Evening review

The review follows Seneca's precedent but uses non-judicial language to reduce
shame and rumination.

Suggested questions:

1. What choice today is worth preserving?
2. Where did an impression or impulse outrun judgment?
3. What is one small adjustment—not a punishment—for next time?

The review should have a two-to-five-minute option, a skip option, and a clear
ending. Repeated distress or rumination should cause the app to suggest a
different family, not a harsher review.

### 10. Study-to-action

Short source excerpts can introduce a concept, but completion requires applying
or testing it. The app should avoid decontextualized quote cards.

Example challenge:

> Read a short explanation of Epictetus's archer analogy. Choose one result you
> care about today, define what “shooting well” means before the result is known,
> and do one part of that process.

### 11. Safe voluntary simplicity

Ancient Stoics sometimes rehearsed hardship. This family is optional and should
be translated conservatively as **voluntary simplicity**, not danger or
deprivation.

Possible challenges include using an adequate item instead of upgrading,
choosing a simpler leisure option, or postponing a convenience for a short
period. Exclude food restriction, medication changes, cold or heat exposure,
sleep restriction, dangerous exercise, deliberate financial insecurity, and
anything likely to interact with illness or disability.

The philosophical purpose must be explicit: learn that a convenience is useful
without treating it as necessary for dignity or good action.

## A proposed starter sequence

This sequence samples the full framework before personalization. It is not a
streak requirement and can be interleaved with the other Daily Forge areas.

| CH-P-003 | Action             | Justice          | Keep or proactively renegotiate one small commitment.                                                       |
| CH-P-004 | Assent             | Temperance       | Pause before one nonurgent reply and name the impulse present.                                              |
| CH-P-005 | Action             | Courage          | Ask one useful low-stakes question you might normally avoid.                                                |
| CH-P-006 | Action             | Justice          | Listen, summarize, and confirm before adding your view in one conversation.                                 |
| CH-P-007 | Assent + action    | Practical wisdom | Name a reversible decision, its main uncertainty, and a good-enough next choice.                            |

The initial completion rule is **attempted the named practice**, not “felt calm,”
“made the right decision,” or “received a positive response.” The reflection is
optional. Challenges involving another person count when the user makes a
respectful offer or fulfills their part, even if the other person declines or
the outcome is disappointing.

## Progression and personalization

Character progression is less linear than physical load. Difficulty depends on
context, relationship, power, emotional charge, ambiguity, and consequence. The
app should not automatically turn a successful exercise into a more intense
interpersonal challenge.

### Progression dimensions

Progress one dimension at a time:

- **timing:** retrospective notice → prepared notice → in-the-moment notice;
- **consistency:** once → repeated in similar contexts → available across
  contexts;
- **complexity:** one fact/value → several competing considerations;
- **behavior:** reflection only → small action → complete bounded action;
- **relationship:** private choice → low-stakes interaction → meaningful
  interaction selected by the user;
- **integration:** one discipline/virtue → coordinated judgment and action; and
- **recovery:** notice after a lapse → repair sooner → prepare for recurrence.

Intensity and consequence should be user-selected, not inferred from a streak.

### Signals for keeping, adapting, or retiring a challenge

- **Keep or repeat:** stretching but manageable, relevant, and useful.
- **Simplify:** user reports overwhelm, ambiguity, rumination, or no safe
  opportunity.
- **Change context:** the practice is understood but the prompt is repeatedly
  irrelevant.
- **Integrate:** the user can perform the isolated skill and wants to apply it to
  an actual commitment.
- **Retire:** the user marks it unwelcome, unsafe, moralizing, or unhelpful.
- **Never escalate automatically:** conflict, disclosure, boundary-setting,
  repair, voluntary discomfort, or any action with material consequences.

### Asymmetry is expected

A user may show courage at work but avoid difficult family conversations; may
be temperate with spending but impulsive in speech; or may judge carefully yet
fail to act. Store context-specific observations rather than one global
“courage level” or “character level.”

## Completion, reflection, and the private Wall

Character entries on the Wall of Goodness should celebrate deliberate practice
without claiming moral certification.

Useful snapshot fields include:

- challenge identifier and version;
- primary and secondary discipline;
- primary and supporting virtue;
- linked Character goal identifiers;
- practice structure: prepare, notice, act, or review;
- user-selected context tag, if any;
- completion account in the user's words;
- optional reflection;
- difficulty and usefulness feedback; and
- whether another person's response was outside the completion rule.

Do not store another person's name, sensitive circumstances, quoted private
speech, or identifying detail by default. Reflection prompts should explicitly
say to describe the user's own choice while omitting identifying information.

Recommended reflection prompts include:

- What did you notice before choosing?
- Which part was yours to do?
- What consideration mattered most?
- What action did you attempt?
- What did the result teach without defining the worth of the action?
- What would you preserve or adjust next time?

Avoid prompts such as “Were you virtuous?”, “How good were you?”, or “Why did
you fail?”

## Selection rules for a future deterministic engine

Runtime selection must remain deterministic and non-AI, consistent with ADR 0006. A reviewed catalog can use explicit metadata and rules.

A future selector should:

1. exclude challenges the user has blocked or whose safety/context requirements
   are not met;
2. avoid repeating the same discipline, virtue, situation, or interaction too
   frequently;
3. prefer incomplete baseline probes until there is enough context, without
   forcing them;
4. alternate inward reflection with outward appropriate action;
5. prioritize justice when recent practice is disproportionately inward;
6. offer lower-intensity alternatives for courage and interpersonal prompts;
7. use self-reported difficulty and usefulness, not streak length, to adjust;
8. avoid pairing a demanding Character interaction with a demanding Community
   challenge on the same day;
9. never infer permission for disclosure, confrontation, repair contact, or
   hardship; and
10. record why a challenge was eligible and selected for auditability.

Offline AI may later analyze a minimized, user-approved export and propose
catalog or rule changes. Those proposals remain untrusted until schema
validation, deterministic safety checks, human review, versioning, tests, and
explicit import, as required by ADR 0006.

## Safety and ethical guardrails

Every Character challenge must satisfy these rules:

1. **No moral score.** Do not calculate virtue points, a goodness percentage, a
   Stoic rank, or a character level.
2. **No shame mechanics.** Missing, replacing, or declining a challenge has no
   punitive language or loss of lifetime accomplishments.
3. **No emotional suppression.** Never equate hiding distress, avoiding help,
   or appearing calm with success.
4. **No diagnosis or therapy claim.** Challenges do not treat anxiety,
   depression, trauma, addiction, anger disorders, or other conditions.
5. **No unsafe hardship.** Exclude food, medication, sleep, temperature,
   dangerous exercise, financial, and exposure experiments that could cause
   harm.
6. **No coerced confrontation.** Do not tell the user to contact, disclose to,
   forgive, reconcile with, or confront another person.
7. **Respect consent.** Help, feedback, discussion, and repair offers must be
   easy to decline. A declined offer can still satisfy the user's practice.
8. **Respect boundaries and power.** Do not frame tolerating mistreatment as
   patience, justice, courage, acceptance, or role duty.
9. **Emergency exception.** Pausing to inspect an impression is for nonurgent
   situations; immediate protective action takes priority in emergencies.
10. **Protect third-party privacy.** The app asks for the user's action, not
    another person's story.
11. **Do not universalize ancient roles.** Translate role ethics through
    contemporary consent, equality, law, reciprocal duty, and legitimate
    renegotiation.
12. **Allow philosophical disagreement.** The user may reject Stoic
    providential metaphysics or a specific ancient claim while using a practical
    exercise.
13. **Prefer proportionate repair.** An apology or contact should serve the
    affected person, not pressure them to relieve the user's guilt.
14. **Keep reflection bounded.** Provide a short option and an exit when review
    becomes rumination.

## Anti-patterns to exclude

- “Do not complain today” without distinguishing concealment from constructive
  communication.
- “Accept everything you cannot control” when advocacy, exit, reporting, or
  protection may be appropriate.
- “Prove courage by doing something frightening.”
- “Skip a meal,” “take a cold shower,” “sleep on the floor,” or other generic
  hardship theater.
- “Call the person who hurt you and forgive them.”
- “Tell someone a difficult truth” without role, consent, timing, safety, or
  proportionality.
- “Help a stranger” in a way that invites surveillance, paternalism, danger, or
  intrusion.
- “Win the argument calmly.”
- “Do not feel angry.”
- “Act like Marcus Aurelius” or imitate an admired figure instead of exercising
  judgment in the user's actual context.
- Decontextualized quotations presented as commands.
- Escalation based on streak, completion count, or a private reflection
  interpreted by the runtime.

The challenge model will eventually need reviewed Character metadata:

- `characterGoalIds`;
- `primaryDiscipline` and optional `secondaryDisciplines`;
- `primaryVirtue` and optional `supportingVirtues`;
- `practiceStructure`;
- expected deliberate minutes;
- context requirements and exclusions;
- interaction level: private, low-stakes social, meaningful social, or civic;
- consequence level and whether explicit user selection is required;
- a lower-intensity variation;
- completion criterion based on process/action;
- reflection prompt;
- consent and third-party privacy note;
- contraindications or safety note;
- catalog source and review version; and
- progression dimensions supported.

The user profile should store preferences and observations, not moral labels:

- favored or blocked practices;
- contexts the user voluntarily selects;
- easy / stretching / too much feedback;
- usefulness feedback;
- whether an opportunity commonly occurs;
- most recently practiced disciplines and virtues;
- unwanted repetition window;
- reflection preference; and
- “never suggest interpersonal challenge escalation” choices.

## Research limitations and open questions

1. Ancient sources disagree in interpretation and survive unevenly. Epictetus's
   three-disciplines schema is influential but should not be presented as the
   only possible account of Stoic practice.
2. The relation Pierre Hadot draws among the three disciplines, the divisions
   of philosophy, and the four virtues is influential but contested in details.
3. Stoic Week's public reports are useful but do not establish long-term causal
   character change.
4. Recent SABS research improves construct validity but does not justify using
   a questionnaire as a personal moral assessment.
5. The user has not yet identified the relationships, recurring situations,
   religious commitments, or non-Stoic ethical traditions that should shape
   personalization.
6. It remains to decide whether Character should usually be a single episode or
   optionally pair morning preparation with evening review.
7. It remains to decide how much source material belongs in the app versus
   linked explanatory pages.
8. Community overlap needs an explicit routing rule when a character exercise
   becomes volunteering or sustained civic participation.
9. A future review should include perspectives critical of Stoicism and people
   with trauma, disability, caregiving, or minority-stress experience before
   interpersonal challenges ship broadly.

## Bottom line

Daily Forge should not try to make the user resemble an impassive Roman statue.
Its Character area should help the user become more deliberate, fair,
courageous, self-governing, teachable, and reliable in ordinary choices.
Epictetus's three disciplines provide the most coherent engine found for that
work; the four virtues keep it balanced; and Stoic Week shows how serious ideas
can become modest daily practices. The app's success criterion remains the
user's honest attempt at a fitting process and action—not serenity, praise,
obedience, a favorable outcome, or a score.
