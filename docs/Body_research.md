# Body challenge research

Research date: 2026-10-31

## Purpose and scope

This document collects evidence and product implications for Daily Forge's
**Body** challenges. The assumed user is over 50, in reasonably good health,
and already follows a disciplined running plan. Body challenges should support,
not replace or disrupt, that plan.

The long-term aims are to help the user:

- retain useful muscle and strength;
- preserve mobility and balance;
- perform ordinary tasks independently into their 80s and 90s;
- reduce age-related loss of muscle function;
- support bone health and reduce fall and fracture risk; and
- retain the physical reserve needed for stairs, carrying, reaching, getting up
  from a chair, and getting up from the floor.

This is product research, not an exercise prescription or medical advice. A
daily prompt cannot diagnose osteoporosis, sarcopenia, balance impairment, or
any other condition, and it cannot safely reproduce a supervised strength or
rehabilitation program.

## Executive summary

1. **Strength work is foundational.** US and international guidance recommends
   muscle-strengthening work involving all major muscle groups on at least two
   days each week. Resistance training improves strength and physical function
   in older adults and can help slow loss of muscle mass.
2. **Function should drive challenge design.** Challenges should map to
   capabilities such as rising from a chair, climbing stairs, carrying
   groceries, lifting an object to a shelf, maintaining balance, and getting up
   from the floor—not to arbitrary exercise novelty.
3. **Balance deserves its own repeated exposure.** Balance and multicomponent
   strength/balance programs improve function and reduce falls. Balance prompts
   should be frequent, progressive, and performed near stable support.
4. **Muscle power matters as well as maximal strength.** Power—the ability to
   produce force quickly—declines with age and is relevant to standing up,
   climbing a step, and recovering balance. Low-risk power challenges may be
   useful after the user has demonstrated control, but explosive or loaded
   movements should not be default unsupervised prompts.
5. **Bone responds to meaningful loading, not merely movement.** Resistance and
   weight-bearing or impact loading can support bone health. The most striking
   bone-density results come from progressive, relatively heavy, supervised
   programs; they do not justify random heavy lifts or jumps in an app.
6. **Mobility should be useful and active.** Ankle, hip, thoracic, and shoulder
   range of motion should be tied to tasks such as squatting, stepping,
   reaching, and floor transfers. Gentle flexibility can support daily
   function, but flexibility-only activity does not replace strength or balance
   work.
7. **The app should offer small practices, not pretend to be a program.** A
   challenge can reinforce a safe movement, add a small amount of volume, or
   encourage a functional practice. Long-term adaptation still requires
   progressive, repeated training. Daily Forge should say this plainly.

## What the principal sources say

### Public-health guidance

The US _Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans_ recommends that adults do
muscle-strengthening activity of moderate or greater intensity involving all
major muscle groups on at least two days per week. For older adults it adds
multicomponent activity that includes balance, aerobic, and muscle-strengthening
work. Effort should be judged relative to the person's fitness, and chronic
conditions should inform what can be done safely.

The National Institute on Aging gives the functional rationale: strength work
supports independence and tasks such as standing from a chair, climbing stairs,
and carrying groceries. It also recommends balance work and describes standing
on one foot, heel-to-toe walking, sideways or backward walking, and chair stands
as examples. It advises stable support when balance is uncertain.

Daily Forge will deliberately leave aerobic programming to the user's running
plan. That is a product boundary, not a claim that aerobic fitness is
unimportant.

### Resistance training, muscle, and strength

The 2026 American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) resistance-training summary
emphasizes that consistency matters more than complicated methods, that bands,
bodyweight, and home-based work can be effective, and that momentary muscular
failure is not required for the average healthy adult. It distinguishes goals:
heavier loading favors maximal strength, greater weekly volume favors muscle
growth, and moderate loads moved quickly can train power.

Systematic reviews in older adults, including adults with sarcopenia and the
"oldest old," generally find that progressive resistance training improves
strength and physical performance. Effects on muscle size vary by population
and program, but resistance training is the most defensible exercise basis for
challenges intended to counter loss of muscle function.

Product implication: a few unprogressed repetitions selected at random are
unlikely to produce the same adaptations as a structured program. Challenges
should either complement the user's established strength work or form part of a
deliberately progressive sequence with recovery and recent workload considered.

### Power and rapid force production

A meta-analysis of randomized trials in adults aged 50 and older found that
resistance training improves lower-extremity muscle power, with high-velocity
training producing larger power gains than traditional slow training in the
included studies. A later review found that power training can improve power
and activity-based performance more than traditional strength training,
although optimal prescriptions remain an active research question.

Product implication: after a safe foundation is established, some challenges
can cue a controlled but purposeful rising phase—for example, standing from a
chair "smoothly and with intent," followed by a slow, controlled return. The app
should not use "as fast as possible," ballistic loading, jumps, throws, or
explosive kettlebell work as general default prompts.

### Balance, falls, and lower-body function

The evidence for exercise reducing falls is strongest for programs that include
balance and functional work, often combined with resistance training. CDC's
STEADI materials use the 30-second chair stand to assess leg strength and
endurance and a four-stage balance test to assess static balance. These are
clinical assessment tools, not daily competitions. Daily Forge can borrow the
movement patterns, but should not diagnose fall risk or encourage users to chase
normative scores.

Product implication: balance challenges should use short, repeatable holds or
steps, offer hand support, stop before form deteriorates, and progress from
static to dynamic tasks. A user unable to hold a tandem stance safely, or who
reports falls or unexplained unsteadiness, should be directed to a clinician or
evidence-based fall-prevention program rather than given harder app challenges.

### Bone health

Bone-health guidance consistently emphasizes two complementary strategies:

- resistance or muscle-strengthening work; and
- weight-bearing or impact loading, when appropriate.

The LIFTMOR randomized trial is important but easy to misuse. In screened
postmenopausal women with low bone mass, eight months of twice-weekly,
high-intensity resistance and impact training improved lumbar-spine and
femoral-neck measures and physical function compared with low-intensity home
exercise. The intervention used loads above 85% of one-repetition maximum and
was **supervised**. It is evidence that appropriately supervised heavy loading
can be valuable; it is not evidence that an app should prescribe unsupervised
heavy deadlifts, squats, overhead presses, or drop landings.

The UK "Strong, Steady and Straight" consensus recommends resistance and impact
exercise for bone strength, strength and balance work to reduce falls, and
spinal-extension work for posture. It also recommends caution with high degrees
of spinal flexion, particularly for people with vertebral fracture or high
fracture risk. Other osteoporosis guidance emphasizes tailoring load and impact
to the individual and prioritizing balance, functional, and resistance work.

Product implication: Daily Forge can safely emphasize loaded carries,
sit-to-stands, step-ups, calf raises, pushing, pulling, hip-hinge skill, and
posture. Impact challenges require explicit eligibility and should not be
enabled by default. Because the target user already runs, additional impact may
also be redundant; this is an inference that should be tested against the
user's actual training and bone-health history.

### The Centenarian Decathlon framing

Peter Attia's useful product contribution is "backcasting": define what a
person wants to be able to do late in life, translate each task into physical
capacities, and train enough reserve now to allow for future decline. Examples
on his personal list include carrying 10 pounds of groceries in each hand up
three flights of stairs, lifting a 30-pound suitcase overhead, getting up from
the floor with one point of support, squatting to lift a 30-pound child, and
getting out of a pool without a ladder.

This is a motivating framework, not a validated clinical protocol. The specific
events and target loads are personal. Daily Forge should use the framework while
grounding challenge safety and frequency in established exercise guidance.

Attia repeatedly emphasizes three ideas that are especially relevant to Daily
Forge:

1. **Specificity:** "exercise more" is not a sufficiently precise long-term
   objective. The user needs concrete events that describe what physical
   participation in later life should look like.
2. **Backcasting:** begin with the desired capability in the final decade and
   work backward to present training needs, allowing for probable decline.
3. **Personal relevance:** the events are not universal. They should reflect the
   user's expected daily life, relationships, travel, hobbies, and environment.

#### What is core and what is elective

- B80-01 through B80-07 form a proposed general independence set, subject to
  medical and environmental constraints.
- B80-08 should use the user's preferred real-world strategy rather than impose
  standing dressing as a moralized ideal.
- B80-09 is elective and environment-specific.
- B80-10 is required as a **design slot**, but its content is chosen by the
  user.
- Loads in B80-03 through B80-06 are provisional defaults. They should be
  configurable by body size, goals, current ability, health constraints, and
  professional guidance.

#### Goal quality requirements

Before an event becomes part of a user's plan, it should specify:

- target age and why the event matters;
- object, load, starting position, finishing position, and environment;
- repetitions, distance, flights, or duration;
- support and assistance permitted;
- movement-quality and stopping conditions;
- whether each side must be assessed separately;
- a safer assessment version;
- component capacities and prerequisite movements; and
- an opt-out or replacement when the event is irrelevant or unsafe.

### Proposed age-60 reserve targets, version 0.1

B60 targets are present-day reserve-capacity goals backcast from B80. They are
not assumptions about the user's current ability and are not the first
challenges the app should issue.

Longitudinal research reports substantial variation in age-related decline.
One study found average knee strength losses of roughly 14–16% per decade,
while other evidence suggests muscle power may decline faster in older age.
Those observations justify building reserve, but they do not provide a precise
personal forecast. For this first planning model, loaded B60 goals generally use
about 1.5 times the B80 load, repetition goals use roughly twice the B80
requirement, and supported tasks move one step toward less support. These are
transparent product assumptions that must be revised using actual performance.

The list is intentionally not limited to ten events. B60-10 through B60-13
decompose the user's personal participation goals rather than hiding them in one
generic slot. Additional personally meaningful goals should receive additional
B60 identifiers.

### Baseline probe protocol

P60 probes discover the user's present capacity relative to a B60 target. The
identifier `P60-XX-YY` means probe `YY` for target `B60-XX`.

Probes are **calibration, not pass/fail challenges**:

- An inability or decision not to attempt a probe is useful information. It
  must not break a streak or produce failure language.
- Begin at the first probe unless existing verified information justifies
  another starting point.
- Offer only one materially demanding probe in a day and consider the user's
  running, strength work, fatigue, soreness, and environment.
- Stop the ladder at the first painful, unstable, technically uncertain, or
  near-maximal result. Do not offer the next probe merely because the prior one
  was completed.
- Target-level probes are offered only after lower probes are comfortable and
  repeatable. High-risk or environment-specific probes require the specified
  supervision.
- Test left and right separately when a task depends meaningfully on one side.
- The highest comfortable observation is an estimated baseline, not permission
  to prescribe that load as routine training.

Every probe should record:

- attempted, completed, modified, or stopped;
- exact load, repetitions, height, support, side, and environment;
- perceived difficulty: very easy, easy, appropriate, hard, or maximal;
- pain or unusual symptoms and when they occurred;
- steady, somewhat unsteady, or unsafe;
- user confidence in repeating the task; and
- later-day and next-day response before load progression.

## Capability map for Daily Forge

The Body library should be organized around future capabilities rather than a
loose collection of exercises.

| Future capability                  | Supporting capacities                                    | Suitable challenge patterns                                                               |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| Get down to and up from the floor  | Hip and ankle mobility, leg and arm strength, sequencing | Supported half-kneel transitions; segmented floor-transfer practice near stable furniture |
| Recover balance                    | Foot/ankle strength, stepping reactions, dynamic balance | Tandem stance; supported single-leg stance; controlled side and backward steps            |
| Walk safely over uneven ground     | Ankle mobility, lateral strength, balance                | Ankle rocks; low obstacle step-over; supported lateral steps                              |
| Reach, dress, and use high shelves | Shoulder and thoracic mobility, overhead control         | Wall slides; comfortable overhead reach; thoracic rotation without load                   |
| Maintain posture under load        | Back extensor endurance, trunk stability                 | Wall posture hold; bird-dog regression; light carry with tall posture                     |

## Recommended challenge families

These are research-derived patterns, not final challenge copy.

### 1. Strength accumulation

- Accumulate a modest number of sit-to-stands during the day.
- Accumulate incline push-ups using a stable surface.
- Complete controlled calf raises near support.
- Complete low step-ups on a stable step, alternating sides.
- Practice a hip hinge to a wall without external load.
- Carry two familiar, manageable household objects for several short trips.
- Use a familiar resistance band for rows if the user has already confirmed
  equipment and technique.

The tracker can count repetitions or sets. Every challenge needs a regression,
a clear stopping rule, and an instruction not to train to failure.

### 2. Balance and foot/ankle capacity

- Hold a staggered stance near a counter for several short bouts.
- Progress to heel-to-toe stance with fingertips available for support.
- Practice supported single-leg standing on each side.
- Take controlled sideways steps beside a counter.
- Step over a low, non-trip object with support nearby.
- Perform slow ankle rocks and toe/heel raises.

Balance duration should be accumulated rather than treated as a maximal test.
Eyes-closed tasks, unstable devices, and unsupervised perturbations are poor
defaults.

### 3. Useful mobility

- Spend several minutes on comfortable ankle dorsiflexion rocks.
- Move through a supported squat-to-chair range without forcing depth.
- Practice a comfortable half-kneeling hip-flexor position with support.
- Perform wall slides or comfortable overhead reaches.
- Practice gentle thoracic rotation in a stable seated or standing position.
- Rehearse one segment of a floor transfer near stable furniture.

Mobility challenges should describe the task they support and should never ask
the user to force range or stretch into pain.

### 4. Low-risk power preparation

- Perform a small number of chair rises with a purposeful rise and slow return.
- Perform low step-ups with a smooth, purposeful upward phase and controlled
  descent.
- Rise onto the toes purposefully and lower slowly while holding support.

These should be offered only after the same movement is already comfortable at
normal speed. They should use low repetition counts and generous rest. Loaded
explosive work belongs in supervised or explicitly programmed training, not in
a surprise daily challenge.

### 5. Bone-supportive loading

- Use resistance-based challenges from the strength family.
- Use familiar loaded carries with conservative loads.
- Include posture and back-extensor work.
- Treat impact, hopping, jumping, and high-load axial exercises as a separate,
  screened tier rather than ordinary challenges.

The app must not claim that completing one challenge increases bone density.
Bone adaptation is site-specific, gradual, and dependent on sufficient,
progressive loading over time.

## Selection and progression rules

### Challenges need memory

The selection engine should consider:

- the user's running and strength work that day;
- muscle groups and movement patterns challenged recently;
- soreness, pain, fatigue, and recovery preference;
- equipment and stable support available;
- known joint, bone, cardiovascular, neurological, or balance constraints;
- whether the user has previously completed the base movement safely; and
- the user's chosen late-life capability goals.

Random variety is less important than repeated, progressive exposure. A useful
progression is usually one variable at a time: more repetitions, slightly more
time, a little more range, less hand support, or a familiar additional load.
The app should not progress load, speed, range, instability, and volume at once.

### Suggested safety tiers

**Tier A — broadly accessible:** movement breaks, ankle rocks, wall slides,
supported stance, low-volume sit-to-stands, calf raises, and incline push-ups
with regressions.

**Tier B — requires demonstrated base capacity:** step-ups, light loaded carries,
supported floor-transfer segments, band resistance, purposeful faster rising,
and longer single-leg holds.

**Tier C — requires explicit screening or professional programming:** jumping,
hopping, drop landings, heavy resistance, loaded spinal movement, explosive
external loads, unstable-surface work, and maximal or near-maximal tests.

Tier C should normally be excluded from Daily Forge's general challenge feed.

## Safety and language requirements

Every Body challenge should:

- provide an easier variation and allow replacement without penalty;
- name required equipment and stable support;
- specify a conservative target and whether it may be split across the day;
- use pain-free, controlled range of motion;
- tell the user to stop for sharp or increasing pain, dizziness, chest pain,
  unusual breathlessness, or loss of balance;
- avoid maximal testing and competition with age-based norms;
- avoid promising prevention, treatment, or reversal of osteoporosis or
  sarcopenia;
- avoid framing support, lighter resistance, or reduced range as failure; and
- avoid aerobic volume, pace, mileage, and intervals because the user's running
  plan owns those decisions.

The app should seek clinical or physical-therapy guidance before offering more
than Tier A when the user reports recent falls, unexplained unsteadiness,
osteoporosis or osteopenia, fragility fracture, joint replacement, significant
pain, neurological symptoms, uncontrolled cardiovascular disease, or a recent
operation or injury.

## Data fields the challenge model will eventually need

In addition to the existing title, instructions, duration, and safety note:

- primary capacity: strength, power, balance, mobility, bone-supportive loading,
  or recovery;
- movement pattern: squat, hinge, push, pull, carry, step, reach, rotate, floor
  transfer, calf/ankle, or posture;
- everyday capability supported;
- body regions and muscle groups;
- equipment and support required;
- minimum demonstrated prerequisite;
- safety tier and contraindication tags;
- base dose, regression, and progression;
- target effort and explicit stop rule;
- whether the dose may be accumulated during the day;
- recent-load and running-day conflicts; and
- evidence rationale and source review date.

## Open research and product questions

1. Is the user also following a structured resistance program? If so, Body
   challenges should mostly cover mobility, balance, and low-fatigue practice.
2. What equipment can be assumed: stable chair, counter, resistance bands,
   dumbbells, kettlebell, or gym access?
3. Should onboarding ask about diagnosed osteopenia/osteoporosis, fractures,
   falls, joint replacements, and clinician-imposed restrictions?
4. What exact grandchild-proxy load and carry distance, floor-play duration,
   travel-luggage weights, and hiking pack, distance, elevation, and terrain
   should define B60-10 through B60-13?
5. Can the user safely perform a chair stand, supported tandem stance, step-up,
   incline push-up, hip hinge, and floor transition? These should be observed or
   carefully self-assessed before progression.
6. How should Daily Forge coordinate challenge load with running intensity and
   recovery days without becoming a second training plan?
7. Should challenges form multiweek progressions, or remain low-fatigue
   supplements to a separate strength program?
